The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM): An Introduction  
The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) is one of the most important theories in modern 
finance. Developed in the 1960s by William Sharpe, John Lintner  and others , CAPM helps 
us answer a fundamental question: What return should investors expect for taking on 
risk?  
Think about it this way: if you're going to invest your money in something risky rather than 
keeping it in a safe government bond, you'll want to be compensated for that extra risk. 
But how much compensation is fair? CAPM gives us a framework to calculate this.  
Types of Risk  
Before CAPM, people understood that riskier investments should offer higher returns, but 
they didn't distinguish between different types of risk. CAPM's breakthrough insight is that 
there are two kinds of risk:  
1. Systematic Risk (Market Risk) : Risk that affects the entire market - things like 
economic recessions, inflation, or political instability. You can't eliminate this risk 
through diversification because it impacts everything.  
2. Unsystematic Risk (Specific Risk) : Risk specific to individual companies or 
industries - like a CEO scandal, a product recall, or new competition. This risk 
can be eliminated through diversification.  
The key insight  of the CAPM is that i nvestors should only be compensated for systematic 
risk, because they can eliminate unsystematic risk for free by diversifying their portfolio.  
(Remember this is an important perspective because we want to determine how much 
return the firm needs to offer the capital market (investors) to obtain capital need for 
planned investment projects. ) 
Beta (β) 
Beta (β) is CAPM's measure of systematic risk. It tells us: To what extent does a security ’s 
return move together with the overall market  return? 
(1) β = 1: The security moves exactly with the market. If the market goes up 10%, this 
security typically goes up 10%.  
(2) β > 1: The security is more volatile than the market. If β = 1.5 and the market goes 
up 10%, this security typically goes up 15%.  
(3) β < 1: The security is less volatile than the market. If β = 0.5 and the market goes 
up 10%, this security typically goes up 5%.

More importantly, t he efficient frontier diagram reveals a crucial insight about optimal 
investing. The curved red line represents the efficient frontier - all possible combinations 
of risky assets that offer the highest expected return for each level of risk. However, when 
we introduce a risk -free investment (like government bonds), we can draw a straight line 
from the risk -free rate that just touches (is tangent to) the efficient frontier at one specific 
point - the market portfolio. This straight line is called the Capital  Market Line (CML), and 
it represents the best possible risk -return combinations available to investors.   
The key characteristic  of the Capital Market Line is that it dominates the entire efficient 
frontier of risky assets alone. Any point on this line represents a portfolio combining the 
risk-free asset with the market portfolio in different proportions. Want lower risk? Hold 
more of the risk -free asset and less of the market portfolio. Want higher expected returns? 
Hold less (or even borrow to buy more) of the market portfolio. This is why the market 
portfolio - that single point where the line touches the eff icient frontier - becomes the 
optimal risky portfolio for all investors, regardless of their risk preferences. Every rational 
investor should hold the same mix of risky assets (the market portfolio) and only vary their 
overall risk level by adjusting how much they combine it with the risk -free asset.  
 
The CAPM Formula  
CAPM gives us a simple but powerful equation to calculate the expected return on any 
investment:  
ri = rf + βi × (E[r Mkt] - rf)

Let's break this down:  
 
ri = Expected return on security i (what we're trying to find)  
rf = Risk-free rate (usually government bond yield)  
βi = Beta of security i (its systematic risk)  
E[rMkt] = Expected return on the market portfolio  
E[rMkt] - rf = Market risk premium (extra return for taking market risk)  
 
A Practical Example  
Let's say you're evaluating Microsoft stock:  
- Risk-free rate (r f) = 3%  
- Expected market return (E[r Mkt]) = 8%  
- Microsoft's beta ( βi) = 1.2  
 
Using CAPM:  
ri = 3% + 1.2 × (8% - 3%) = 3% + 1.2 × 5% = 3% + 6% = 9%  
So according to CAPM, investors should expect a 9% return from Microsoft stock to be 
fairly compensated for its risk.  
 
Real-World Applications  
Although the CAPM is not perfect and has been extended over the years (in particular by  
Fama and French, two US scholars), it is used extensively in:  
(1) Corporate Finance: Calculating the cost of equity capital  
(2) Investment Analysis: Determining if stocks are fairly priced  
(3) Portfolio Management: Constructing efficient portfolios  
CAPM is still influential because it provides a logical framework for thinking about risk and 
return. Its core insight - that only systematic risk deserves compensation - remains 
relevant today.
